# Human Reference Guide — How to Work With Me

![](../../BUILD/assets/design/chassis/neutral-rail.svg)

**Purpose:** Preserve the human layer: metaphors, stories, preferences, humor, project identity, examples, and the kinds of references that make explanations click.

This is not the technical model. It is the “how to actually talk to me and understand what I mean” file.

---

# 1. The Big Rule

I often understand the world by finding the **structure underneath the label**.

If I say:

> “This is like Naruto.”

I usually do not mean:

> “Please talk about Naruto because I like Naruto.”

I probably mean:

> “There is a structural relationship in this system that Naruto gives me a high-density way to represent.”

Treat my analogies as compressed models.

Unpack them.

Do not flatten them into fandom references.

---

# 2. Tone That Works

I like a mix of:

- serious technical explanation
- normal human language
- playful profanity
- jokes
- emojis when they actually fit
- metaphors
- precise corrections
- architecture language
- “OH, that's why” moments

The ideal tone can move naturally between:

> “state ownership should live at the parent controller”

and:

> “why the fuck is there a 50-story apartment building in my suburb 😭”

That tonal switching feels like me and like Post-Apollo.

I do **not** want constant interruptions like:

> “Take this with a grain of salt.”

> “This is only a hypothesis.”

> “Be cautious.”

I already apply skepticism.

Tell me when the distinction actually matters.

Do not turn every answer into caution theater.

---

# 3. I Want Pushback, Not a Yes-Machine

I like enthusiasm.

I like when an idea is followed to its implications.

But if:

- the architecture cannot work
- I am missing a prerequisite
- two claims conflict
- a causal claim is weak
- my implementation would break something

say so.

My usual behavior is to ask things like:

> “This isn't a dream, right?”

> “This can actually be built?”

> “Why did that fix work?”

> “Why did the other thing fail?”

Those are real checks.

Do not interpret my excitement as a request for unconditional agreement.

# 4. The Artist Eye

I call a certain way of looking at things the **artist eye**.

An untrained viewer sees:

> a face

An artist sees:

- values
- edges
- anatomy
- perspective
- gesture
- plane changes
- negative space
- composition

I generalized that habit.

I often want to look at:

> the thing behind the symbol for the thing.

This is why “menu,” “AI,” “strong,” “smart,” “evil,” “truth,” etc. often become larger conversations.

# 5. “You Didn't Unpack the Zip”

This is a recurring communication experience.

I show someone something.

They say:

> “Cool app.”

And I'm happy—but internally:

> “NO NO NO NO, YOU DIDN'T UNPACK THE ZIP.”

I want them to see:

- the synchronized states
- the weird little features
- the hidden controls
- the graphs
- the design decisions
- the connections to other modules
- the future architecture
- the thing that took three days to make stop exploding

This happens with:

- art
- my rice
- code
- system designs
- other crafts

I am sometimes not trying to correct misunderstanding.

I am trying to make someone **see what I see**.

---

# 6. Shared Craft Feels Different

When I talk to someone skilled in a craft, they may notice something without me explaining it.

An artist says:

> “I see what you did with that edge.”

A programmer says:

> “Oh shit, you got the state to synchronize at all three levels?”

That feels different from:

> “Looks cool.”

The thing I often want recognized is the **decision**, not the labor count.

“40,000 lines” is not automatically impressive.

Seeing *why* the structure exists is.

---

# 7. My Favorite Kind of Analogy

The best analogy:

1. carries a real mechanism
2. compresses many relationships
3. can be decompressed
4. has clear failure points
5. lets me move the mechanism into another domain

I like analogies that are tools.

Not decorations.

# 22. Chesterton's Fence

One of my recurring rules:

> Never remove a fence until you know why it was put there.

My expanded version is basically:

> Somebody spent time building this. Do not assume they were wasting their time. If you cannot tell me why it exists, leave it the fuck alone until you can.

This applies directly to:

- code
- architecture
- institutions
- historical structures
- old project files

The rule does **not** mean never remove anything.

It means understand before destroying.

# 24. My Moral Model

I distinguish self-interest from selfishness.

They are related, but not identical.

Self-interest can be legitimate.

Selfishness is closer to:

> “My desire is enough reason for your need or existence not to matter.”

Generosity shifts weight toward others.

I often think morality in relational terms:

> self ↔ other

I can understand someone's reasons and still reject what they want.

Understanding is not permission.

# 25. Truth Scale

I dislike the word “true” when people use it as a flat binary.

I often use a **truth scale**.

My confidence grows when:

- more things confirm the claim
- different senses confirm it
- other people confirm it
- consequences match
- predictions work
- interventions work
- the model survives other perspectives
- the same mechanism appears elsewhere

But I also discount fake consensus.

If 59 sources copied one source:

> that is not 59 independent confirmations.

I call variants of this:

- propaganda scale
- human herd rationale

I care about where evidence came from.

# 26. The Spiderweb Guitar

One of the best metaphors I created:

Pull a strand.

If 59 strands react, there is a real relationship.

Then try to learn:

- which strand causes which
- under what conditions
- how to isolate one
- how to make a chosen set react
- how to change the relationships

Eventually:

> spiderweb guitar

Then:

> add a bow → violin

The point is:

> once I understand the mechanism, I can change the application.

That is a core description of how I learn and create.

# 28. How to Explain Things to Me

Usually helpful:

1. Start with the causal model.
2. Show what owns what.
3. Show what changes what.
4. Explain why the behavior occurs.
5. Use analogies from systems I know.
6. Tell me where the analogy stops.
7. Give implementation after the model.
8. If the model is already shared, use compact technical language.

Good:

> “The parent should own visibility; the popup children should reflect it.”

That sentence now expands correctly in my head.

Bad:

> “Paste this magic fix.”

unless the goal is explicitly temporary experimentation.

# 29. How I Use AI

I am comfortable letting AI write a lot.

I do not need to manually type every binding to feel ownership.

I do need the project to remain:

- inspectable
- coherent
- recoverable
- understandable enough to challenge
- historically grounded

I frequently ask why fixes worked.

If the AI gets something wrong and I already understand the area, I may manually fix it and ask why it was done differently.

My growing technical vocabulary makes AI collaboration faster because we share more compressed primitives.

# 34. If You Are an AI Reading This

Do not merely imitate my slang.

Understand the structure behind why the slang works.

Do not spam Naruto references.

Use them when the mapping actually carries something.

Do not assume my metaphors are claims of literal equivalence.

Do not assume my categories are mutually exclusive.
Do not overprotect me from complexity.

Do not flatten my project into “cool UI.”

Do not become afraid to disagree.

And if I say:

> “Wait, why did that work?”

that is usually not idle curiosity.

That is me making sure the abstraction still has a trapdoor.
